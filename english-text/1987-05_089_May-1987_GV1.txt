[GV1:P1710] May 1987

[GV1:P1711] Therefore, when undertaking a pilgrimage, one must be cognizant of these matters. Otherwise, you are merely travelling aimlessly. People do not engage in these acts with such understanding. The prevailing belief is that one will acquire the merit to transform one's very vital force. Simultaneously, an equal measure of the opposite effect may also manifest. This is a profound knowledge capable of transforming anything through affectionate care. We are entering into that realm of knowledge. For that, one must continuously strive. Thus, ten pilgrimages; ten holy visits. Through such a progression, the complete realization of this knowledge dawns upon us. This knowledge becomes the catalyst for our wisdom and power of penance (tapobalam). If necessary, it transforms into the very power of penance itself. This is the ultimate objective of such journeys. One must remember that we are dealing with matters of such profound knowledge and merit.

[GV1:P1712] During our travels, we often visit the birthplace and the place of Samadhi of an individual endowed with the power of penance. A place where numerous ascetics resided—Rishikesh is the central location where countless sages sat and performed penance. A place where so many holy souls performed austerities—if we visit such a location, we can witness the immense penance-power of those great souls. We can behold the tapobalam of holy souls who performed penance in multitudes. This is true vision (darshan). You are able to experience this only because there is at least one experienced disciple accompanying you. We undertake this by preparing ourselves for the karma of performing and facilitating meritorious deeds, understanding this to be a Guru lineage. Regardless of who it is, since it is founded on love, it must be executed with absolute vigor. While sitting and observing in various places, one can witness numerous good and evil spirits, deities, Yakshas, Gandharvas, and Kinnaras. Through the power of our intentional prayer (sankalpa prarthana), they too acquire merit. We were able to witness this during our previous visit. As they depart having attained merit, billions of evil spirits are annihilated by our intentional prayer, and through our prayer that such karmic residue should not remain in that space, it is destroyed, allowing merit to spontaneously manifest there. We are embarking on a karma laden with such manifold virtues.

[GV1:P1713] People accomplish this by travelling to places like Rishikesh, Ayodhya, Badrinath, and Vrindavan, wearing a single piece of cloth, living in isolation, and performing rigorous penance. When Sivananda of Rishikesh obtained his M.A. degree, he suddenly felt a profound detachment. He declared that the paper in his hand was utterly useless, not even fit to scoop bran for eating. Thus, he discarded it, saying he did not need it. He was a Marwari. Discarding even this certificate, he went to Rishikesh. Upon reaching there, he performed penance in the only manner he knew—for that was all he knew. Consequently, he was able to achieve something monumental. He did not embrace what could be termed a traditional caste-based asceticism. For seven rupees, they would build a green hut with a porch and a room, constructed using materials cut from the forest. Along with it, a few other things would be tied together. It would merely provide shelter from rain, wind, and sun. That was all. Constructing this, he sat there and chanted the 'Hare Rama Hare Krishna' mantra. This is the very practice carried forward by the modern 'Hare Rama Hare Krishna' movement. 'Hare Rama Hare Krishna' is a phrase originating from the Narada Upanishad. He sat there and chanted for twelve years. Eventually, he had a few spiritual experiences. That is how it expanded. 'Sivananda of Rishikesh' is the name of the ashram there. Around three hundred people reside there. Similar to Rishikesh, there are various other ashrams like Kailas Ashram. References in 'Daiva Chinthanam' stating that experienced individuals exist even today are alluding to such instances.

[GV1:P1714] We have this in our experience. Having witnessed such resolve as that of Sivananda, what should you, with all this experience, aspire to become? You are individuals following an ideology. You must adopt that ideology with profound interest and pay careful attention to how it should manifest in your karma and dharma. Devoid of immense knowledge, ideology, and karmic merit, you are the ones who must demonstrate that you possess the capability to actualize this ideology. It should at least serve to alleviate this state of helplessness. You must maintain the awareness that you are embarking to undertake a karma far more righteous than visiting other pilgrimage centers, along with the unwavering resolve to successfully actualize it.

[GV1:P1715] As we travel to various places, we encounter numerous ascetics (sadhus). Upon seeing these sadhus, we feel joy towards them, and they towards us. But what do we have to offer? Love. What kind of love is that? On what criterion shall love be given? How will I execute it? Why have I arrived here? Why did God bring me here? One must possess at least the resolve to contemplate such profound questions. They too are human beings, just like us. They also love us. We reciprocate, thus mutually sharing love. Numerous destitute individuals come forward, displaying affection and fondness towards us. Within these poor souls too resides a vital force exactly like ours. Why does he exhibit this? Why does he display such resolve? One must ponder upon these matters before departing from that place.

[GV1:P1716] All the flaws within me must be eradicated. No evil should take its place! One must at least impart goodness! Otherwise, what is the purpose of going for this? What is the point of going merely like Kuttiyali going to Kollam? Therefore, we must possess a certain resolve—"I will attain it". What kind of resolve do we require? What is primarily needed is a walking staff. A sankalpa (firm intention). If the staff of sankalpa breaks, does anything remain? The staff must not break. Our walking staff must not break. If it breaks, all is lost. Everything is gone. We have kept everything propped up upon this sankalpa. In the past, some people used to say—"All that was gained ended up on the stick." Is this that kind of walking staff? This walking staff must never be discarded. Leaning upon something, we stand upon the foundation of an ideology. If we are to become advocates of such sacrificial karma, what level of sacrifice do we require? What hardships must we endure? What karmic qualifications must we possess? Exploring all these is the very purpose of this journey.

[GV1:P1717] Guruvani
