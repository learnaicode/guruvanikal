[GV1:P1180] May 1982

[GV1:P1181] Regardless of one's level of education, a person cannot truly know the Guru. He can merely articulate perspectives by referencing the Puranas and Upanishads. These texts were authored by poets, erudite scholars, and a few individuals with practical experience. However, none of them can accurately write about the true Gurus. An illustrative example is that when studying this Guru, one realizes he has traversed through countless eras across numerous incarnations—as a wanderer, a devotee, a yogi, an enlightened soul, and a man of action. Following all these arduous phases, he incarnates as various forms of Gurus across seven distinct births. These seven births are profoundly destined for attaining the divine presence of God.

[GV1:P1182] This is a matter you must be exceedingly careful about. Through these seven births, they emerge as Gurus in various forms and depart. If not within a single epoch, they take these seven births over the course of seven epochs. Who is capable of writing the history of such a Guru? Can it be accomplished by anyone other than a disciple who has arrived alongside the Guru? It is impossible for anyone else. That is why it is said that when studying the Guru, or while living alongside the Guru, one must carefully preserve the exact words spoken by the Guru. He needs to study nothing else. That itself is a glory. Through seven births as a Guru, he undertook seven kinds of responsibilities, and by the seventh state, he had departed. Subsequently, he is born again only in accordance with the mandate of the Divine Will (*Brahmanishchayam*). We cannot ascertain how the Divine Will is going to be proclaimed. How can one write the history of this person? No one but a disciple who reveals this person can write it. However, suppose that Guru has a thousand experienced disciples. The thousand individuals will perceive it in a thousand different ways. If what is perceived in a thousand ways is documented in a thousand different manners, how will people understand this Guru? It would be akin to an auspicious moment being ruined into a complete mess. Consequently, one cannot truly know this Guru. In reality, very few would even venture to write about such a Guru.

[GV1:P1183] One can write the biography of the Guru. However, it should not be written merely to demonstrate who the Guru is today. That is a perilous undertaking. This Guru might occasionally speak many things without premeditation. There are both positive and negative elements in what is spoken thus impulsively. The reason is that this Guru is not someone who has lived a highly systematic and disciplined life. In the perspective of a scholar, this will be perceived as a deficiency. A ritualist, regardless of who he is, will address him merely as the son of Chanku the butcher. He can only perceive him in that manner. He fails to comprehend why this individual was born as the son of this Chanku. Only by being born in such a manner can he impart certain truths to the world. Had he been born as the son of a wealthy man or a king, a different complication would arise there. The births of the Prophet and Jesus serve as examples of this. But the birth of Moses is not analogous.

[GV1:P1184] The Prophet was born as a remarkably handsome individual and the son of a sculptor. He was also a wealthy person in his life. Furthermore, he was a member belonging to the royal lineage of that land. By the standards of that era, sculptors were not ordinary individuals. Because they meticulously studied this art and elevated it to a prestigious status, it was managed by the kings of that time. However, stating this does not imply that the Prophet did not experience poverty. Poverty was imposed upon him during a particular phase. He was born into this impoverished state, sustaining himself through sculpture, precisely to convey the reality of such hardship. Yet, to impart teachings, there must be one possessing exemplary knowledge. That is why, despite lacking formal education in literacy, he possessed profound knowledge in all these matters. The words of the Prophet are monumental words. The reason for this stems from his distinguished lineage.

[GV1:P1185] Moses was a knowledgeable individual born into a relatively affluent family. He was raised in grandeur, yet learned through the experience of poverty. There is a distinction regarding the birth of Moses. He was fortunate by birth. Having been raised in prosperity, he demonstrated the stark contrast between prosperity and poverty to the people, presenting himself as entirely impoverished so as to be acceptable to the destitute.

[GV1:P1186] Jesus did not arrive in that manner. A highly qualified individual came and spoke, yet you failed to accept it. Therefore, he was ordained to be born in a place devoid of any notable heritage to speak of. When Jesus was brought forth in this way, since the local populace lacked the capacity to accept it, they engaged in conflicts, waged wars, and created sheer turmoil. How he came to be a Guru for us remains a profound question even today. Time elapsed as they continued to ponder this. The kingdom was ultimately destroyed. Studying such figures presents somewhat of a complex conundrum. It is perhaps better to simply believe without deeply analyzing it. Consequently, if we are to explain and comprehend this, it is merely necessary to study their history to some extent.

[GV1:P1187] When we begin to comprehend such great figures, if this is their nature, who would be the previously mentioned 'Guru across epochs' (*Kalanthara Guru*)? How can we possibly encapsulate them in words? This is precisely why Kabir Das, who incarnated as Suka Brahmarshi, articulated it in this manner. That Suka has been undergoing birth and death ever since this human realm came into existence. He intrinsically knows who the Guru is in this world. Only he comprehends what Gurus accomplish when they manifest in the world in this manner. It is said that to document the history of such a Guru, even if all the water in the world's oceans were churned into ink, and all the trees were uprooted to serve as pens, it would remain utterly insufficient. Thus, once the history of one individual is written, there will not be enough 'water' left here to chronicle the history of another.

[GV1:P1188] Guruvani
