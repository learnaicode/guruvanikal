[GV1:P1557] November 1985

[GV1:P1558] The fundamental understanding of an 'Ashram' first emerged during an era when society lacked comprehensive awareness. (Today represents an age where everyone invokes the divine.) Ancient humans possessed greater longevity. Through relentless contemplation, even their vital force would generate immense spiritual heat. Righteous thought and conduct facilitate a balanced, moderate life. Society presumed that individuals leading such lives served as profound exemplars. That particular epoch possessed its own distinct characteristics. Subsequently, times changed. Today, Ashrams have proliferated even in rural villages. In the primitive stages, Ashrams were situated on secluded mountains. Why are Ashrams so ubiquitous in contemporary times? It is an inherent characteristic of the current era. The global population has expanded exponentially. In the past, human actions (*karma*) were driven by deep-rooted latent tendencies (*vasanas*). During those times, the population was sparse, space was abundant, and the intensity of *karma* was relatively subdued. The criteria for eligibility that exist today were absent then. In the modern context, qualifications, *karma*, and ultimate liberation (*mukti*) are often claimed merely by virtue of birth. What can be deduced from this? Even an individual who has merely functioned as a ritualistic practitioner (*karmi*) or lived as a *yogi* possesses this underlying *vasana* (for acquiring true knowledge). However, the manifestation of these tendencies varies significantly among individuals.

[GV1:P1559] We tend to label all such inclinations as devotion (*bhakti*). Minor variations introduced over time gradually evolve into substantial disparities. Profound and rigid distinctions also emerge. By profound distinctions, I refer to the culmination of individual *karma*. In this context, there exist souls that have brought their respective *karmas* to fruition. Conversely, there are souls that have achieved completion in nothing. There are souls that have merely initiated the process. Some have progressed halfway, while others have completed three-quarters of their spiritual journey. Still, others have made only marginal progress. Every one of us operates across these myriad stages of spiritual evolution. Consequently, the latent tendencies (*vasana*) are never uniform across individuals; they vary significantly across the domains of *karma*, *bhakti*, and *jnana* (knowledge). Therefore, for those residing in an ashram, the experience is never monolithic—different individuals acquire distinct forms of knowledge. Even when a collective experience occurs, it is perceived in a thousand different ways by a thousand different people. This principle applies equally to *bhakti*, *karma*, and *yoga*. Minute discrepancies often go unnoticed. Both you and I may perform an action with devotion, yet the magnitude of that devotion varies. Such subtleties can only be discerned through prolonged temporal experience. Consequently, the true essence of ashram life is comprehended solely through sustained duration. Therefore, this understanding must be actively articulated and cultivated amongst us. We have lacked the tradition of such experiential instruction. It is for this reason that devotion continues to stagnate at its primitive stages—akin to the devotion prevalent during the *Treta Yuga*.

[GV1:P1560] The paradigm of devotion characteristic of the *Treta Yuga* is insufficient to attain any elevated spiritual state in the contemporary era. The rationale is that in antiquity, realizing a spiritual experience required extensive epochs. That is no longer the case. Your individual soul (*jiva*) is an entity that has accumulated immense antiquity through a prolonged cycle of births and deaths. This evolutionary attainment manifests in multiple trajectories. One trajectory involves a soul that has acquired—or earned the karmic fortune of—action (*karma*) through devotion, merit (*punya*) through *karma*, and ultimate knowledge (*jnana*) through that merit. Another entity remains anchored in the desire for *karma*, while another yearns exclusively for knowledge. Furthermore, there are souls that have not yet even approached the realm of conscious *karma*; they have never harbored such aspirations.** These are souls that developed an inclination only upon witnessing these phenomena. How, then, can their spiritual progression reach completion? An innumerable multitude exists in precisely such disparate states in the modern era. It is inaccurate to postulate that devotion has diminished in contemporary times. Rather, amidst an intensification of this devotion, each of us is governed by distinct latent tendencies (*vasanas*). The fundamental cause of this divergence is the soul undergoing numerous incarnations, engaging in diverse *karmas* across expansive temporal spans. This includes circumstances of shared collective existence, followed by incarnations scattered across various geographical terrains, nations, and cosmic realms. Philosophical traditions postulate the existence of multiple such realms. Entities incarnated across such vast spectrums eventually converge at a singular spatio-temporal juncture.

[GV1:P1561] There are quite a few people here living as householders. Even your own family members do not like you, do they? What is the reason for that? This phenomenon exists even among children born to the same mother. Shouldn't there be at least some affection in accordance with human values? Whether born to my mother, or an uncle's children, or a father's children, whoever they may be—didn't they grow up together? Shouldn't there be at least an understanding to respect the place where one has gone and not find fault with it? Shouldn't one go there and cooperate? Why doesn't that happen? It has been like this for many births. If someone comes here just to see you, they will say they are not entering inside. This essentially means they do not respect it. Why does that happen? They were born somehow. In those days, he might have been a servant. If he develops an affection for the house, then it is like the children of the same father. They are born like this. He will not get close. Whether male or female. There are some girls. They leave the house in a manner where they never get along with the family. Then they will never return to that house. That is the result of being ruined while working as servants in that house back then. They were born out of desire. After being born, they leave just like that. There are numerous such matters involved in this.

[GV1:P1562] Just because you reside in an ashram, you will not automatically develop prudent care. Without that care, living in an ashram yields no result. So many people have lived alongside Sree Narayana Guru. They understood nothing. There was a time when Narayana Guru lay ill in a choultry. It was only three days prior to his Samadhi. He was crying out in pain, 'Ayyo, ayyo'. Many people assume that Narayana Guru should not experience such pain, or that Chattampi Swami should not experience this. They believe one should die with their head bursting—that is Samadhi. That is how many people perceive it. It is not possible to just burst and leave like that.

[GV1:P1563] This is the Kali Yuga. How do people behave in the Kali Yuga? Gurus will arrive to guide them back onto a proper path. They differ from those who focus solely on their own lives, ascending like a beetle climbing a thread. You do not comprehend this karma. Those who ascend like a beetle on a thread do so solely for the sake of their own lives. There are those who absorb the flaws and burdens of numerous lives. Such a Guru cannot merely sit in one place and practice yoga. He may appear like a servant, or sometimes like a manager. Why is that? Only by acting in this manner can he nourish those standing nearby. Only if he behaves like a servant will such people approach him. Otherwise, they will not come. Sometimes like a scholar, sometimes like a sage, they can be seen manifesting four different characteristics simultaneously. To some, he appears as none of these, but rather like a great farmer. All these traits will manifest in them.

[GV1:P1564] There is a section that describes the Avadhuta. It is not like this. He will behave like a ghost, like a madman. Going among people, he will behave without any distinction between men and women. That can occur even within an ashram. Does someone wandering like a madman possess any discipline? What does it matter to him whether he is in an ashram or at a public feast? To everything, he reacts as if he is mad. It is said they are like the wind and like growing children. Does anyone know which way the wind blows? Growing children will behave in various unpredictable ways. An Atmayogi is described as one who wanders devoid of illusions, devoid of ordinary intellect, and devoid of magic. None of these human illusions will be found in him. There will be no conventional logic. Devoid of intellect and magic—intellect implies a measured mind, a behavior that brings satisfaction to everyone; he does not possess that either. Then there is the complete trickster, the one who demonstrates magic. He doesn't have that either. Then how can one understand him? He might be seen at a public gathering or on the road. How can he be evaluated? Ordinary people cannot evaluate him. He is someone who has ascended solely through a state of yoga.

[GV1:P1565] I will narrate an incident that took place in Neyyattinkara. There was a person named Keshava Sanyasi. He would gather rubbish and garbage lying on the road and tie it into a large bundle. He would place it somewhere and lie down. He would chew betel leaves and walk around with the juice drooling. Some people would come and buy tea for him. It would be tea purchased in the morning, with flies fallen into it. He would remove them and drink it. He would utter various things. Those standing around would realize that he was speaking about them. They were incoherent conversations lacking logical connection. He spoke about the specific matters of each individual standing in the crowd. They would understand it.

[GV1:P1566] Once in Neyyattinkara, a woman in a house suffered from severe stomach pain. She couldn't even be transported to the hospital. He used to visit the neighboring house. The husband of the woman, who was writhing in agony, ran to him and informed him. He went to that house, removed the woman's clothes, embraced her, and rolled on the floor. The husband stood there in a panic. He had called him, what else could he do? When the locals learned of this, they arrived armed with wooden logs and palm fronds. Then this man slowly stood up and said, "It is done, it is all done," and simply walked away. The woman's stomach pain vanished. What do we understand from this? The dirt rolled from their sweat is termed 'Ramakkatti'. Only sanyasis know of it. This sweat, this dirt, this saliva—if all this rubs off from their body, it is considered a medicine. For this, one should remain unbathed. If he bathes, this will not be obtained. Isn't it washed away by daily bathing? Then only the fresh sweat will be available, right? If they remain unbathed with the sweat, just rolling this dirt from it and administering it is sufficient for the patient. This is called Ramakkatti. If you hear this, you might express disgust. Their sweat and dirt cure illnesses. Why is this mentioned? That is the conduct of an avadhuta. It is for those who serve as such messengers. Through ashram life, we must comprehend each of these concepts. This must also be imparted to society. For that, those residing in the ashram must understand these things prudently. There are many who travel upon the path of self-realization. If one lacks prudence, they might unknowingly insult such great souls. That will lead to profound destruction.
