[GV1:P1255] February 1983

[GV1:P1256] What is meant by Yoga? The term possesses multifaceted meanings. Yoga is synonymous with fortune. There exists Yoga achieved through action (karma). Within traditional medicine, a specific oil preparation is referred to as a yoga, just as an electuary (lehyam) is termed a yoga. Thus, Yoga manifests in numerous forms. Accordingly, there once existed a monarchical reign here led by a yogi who attained his state through karma. Aspiring for benevolence, this yogi executed commendable deeds within his administration and garnered renown. Amidst this acclaim, he was struck by the realization, "I now require a degree of divinity as well." He prepared himself to transition toward that pursuit. Consequently, he began to experiment with substances ranging from medicinal herbs to the navapashanas (nine toxic minerals). The formulation (yoga) for this was also devised by a fellow yogi. That specific yoga subsequently evolved into atmavidya (spiritual knowledge).

[GV1:P1257] Today, scarcely anyone recognizes that it is, in fact, a structured discipline (vidya). Nor does it inherently possess divinity. True yoga fundamentally equates to fortune. However, the aforementioned ascetic or sovereign, through rigorous practice, exhibited certain mystical powers (siddhis) and an aura of divinity. That era marked the zenith of the Devas, encompassing the Treta Yuga and Dvapara Yuga. It was an epoch where a Deva possessed the absolute capability to execute or compel any action. Should a Deva manifest, they could display an array of extraordinary phenomena on this earth, thereby persuading the populace of their benevolence. It was an age where grandeur was intertwined even within the guise of a demon. In that context, such phenomena held significant relevance. Ultimately, this transformed into their yoga, and subsequently, their defining qualification.

[GV1:P1258] The aforementioned yogi, or monarch, commenced his yogic practices within a massive walled enclosure encompassing approximately fifty acres of land. To facilitate this, a select group of his administrative subordinates adopted an exceptionally ascetic lifestyle. However, the rulers of that era also possessed considerable administrative prowess. Having governed proficiently, they issued an authoritative mandate stipulating, "The administration shall proceed in this manner; it must be conducted according to these specific protocols," before retreating to pursue yogic disciplines themselves. They dedicated extensive periods to this endeavor. Consequently, they acquired the capacity to live for remarkably prolonged durations. Even as entire populations perished and faded away, these individuals managed to evade death.

[GV1:P1259] Our physiology possesses a predetermined quota for the number of breaths it can expend. They devised a specialized technique to regulate this respiratory cycle and, furthermore, to propel the physical body beyond its natural biological constraints. Concurrently, they became impervious to the afflictions of senescence and physical deterioration. This was achieved by fundamentally altering the body's metabolic processes. In order to preserve their physical form, they consumed exceptionally nutritious and fortifying sustenance. Thus, through this kalpaseva (rejuvenation therapy), the body would be systematically conditioned to a state where it required neither a single drop of water nor the smallest fragment of foliage to sustain itself. Upon the completion of each phase of this regimen, the practitioner would shed a layer of skin, accompanied by the complete loss of body hair. Following the subsequent phase, the entire physical structure—including flesh, bones, and marrow—would dissolve into a seemingly amorphous mass of tissue. At this juncture, the physical appearance would be exceedingly grotesque, with the eyes becoming indistinguishable, descending into a state akin to profound comatose oblivion. Nevertheless, throughout this agonizing metamorphosis, the disciples who had steadfastly revered and believed in him would maintain a vigilant perimeter, while his consort would remain intimately by his side, providing care as meticulous and tender as that bestowed upon a newborn infant.

[GV1:P1260] At that stage, the entire skeletal structure renders as fragile and tender as that of a nascent avian hatchling. Subsequently, it undergoes a profound reconfiguration, adapting to a completely novel physiological paradigm. This state is meticulously cultivated directly from the forces of nature. As the body progressively consolidates its new form, the necessity for physical nourishment steadily diminishes. Consequently, the individual transcends the original frail physical frame and graying hair, emerging with an entirely regenerated constitution. While the fundamental morphological outline of the body and the subtle cognitive faculties of the mind remain intact, virtually every other biological attribute is irrevocably transformed. The practitioner no longer requires rudimentary physiological functions such as excretion. The entire biological mechanism transitions into an existence sustained by an entirely distinct elemental force.

[GV1:P1261] Thereafter, he requires absolutely nothing from the natural world. His sole imperatives are to engage in profound meditation or to omnipresently observe the cosmos through his attained mystical clairvoyance (siddhi). Each practitioner systematically evolved into this transcendent state. Remaining seated atop a block of solid ice would impart no physiological distress. Thus, he possessed the liberty to traverse any region across the globe, ascending the most treacherous mountains or navigating the densest, most formidable forests. He could handle any venomous serpent with impunity; should a serpent strike him, the creature itself would perish, whereas his physical form would remain entirely unscathed. In fact, wild beasts would instinctually flee before even crossing his path. A prevailing conviction also emerged that his mere intention possessed the destructive potential to reduce anything to ashes. Paradoxically, these individuals were utterly incapable of executing tangible actions or dispensing practical counsel for the betterment of humanity. Having glimpsed the Supreme Consciousness, and subsequently transcending rudimentary awareness, such a being perceives this universal spirit within all entities and exists in a state of absolute, passive stasis. He is devoid of the capacity to engage in proactive earthly endeavors. It is maintained that this phenomenon holds true even in the contemporary era; it is not deemed a fallacy.

[GV1:P1262] The Divine provides us with an observable precedent for this phenomenon within nature itself. Analogous to this grand scale of asceticism, entities such as serpents, amphibians, ants, and termites engage in their own profound metamorphosis. The sprouting of wings and the transformative emergence of butterflies are all direct biological culminations analogous to the immense power derived from such rigorous ascetic disciplines.

[GV1:P1264] March 1983

[GV1:P1265] A wasp is often compared to a Sanyasi. It brings a caterpillar or something similar, stings it in various parts of its body to paralyze it (without killing it), and places it at the entrance of its nest. Then, it inspects all four sides of the nest, brings suitable mud to seal it, and pushes the prey inside. Afterward, it closes the nest with the mud, inserts its antenna inside, and buzzes. Imprinting this sound inside, it follows a specific sequence—usually three or seven times—buzzing around the nest. Then it departs from there. When the appropriate time arrives, this creature or caterpillar inherently assumes the form of a wasp. Its intention and action have yielded results; a creation has occurred. This is its creation.

[GV1:P1266] Similarly, through the science of Yoga, by undertaking *kalpaseva* (prolonged spiritual discipline), a new creation is brought forth. For yogis, this is not termed Sanyasa. It is called Yoga. Yoga is attained through *Tapas* (penance). Sanyasa is not like that. Instead of pursuing the path of Sanyasa, these yogis isolated themselves and commenced penance. Thus, they withdrew to the forest with the conviction that by exhausting all actions (*karmas*), the soul would attain liberation. To a certain extent, this can be referred to as Sanyasa. However, a true Sanyasi is not that. One who is considered a Sanyasi is inherently pure and holy from birth. Yet, when he resides among us, we may fail to recognize that greatness. When our father lives with us, we do not comprehend his profound greatness; we only recognize his love. We merely acknowledge their obligations, assuming that our parents are duty-bound to provide everything for us. We seldom reflect upon their true greatness.

[GV1:P1267] Sanyasa is of four types: Karma, Yoga, Jnana, and Bhakti. Among these, the true Sanyasi is the *Jnani* (the enlightened one). He remains aligned with all actions. He will not reject any action. Furthermore, he has no desire to accumulate or preserve anything specifically for his physical body. He will act somewhat in accordance with human dharma. He will let certain things take their course according to destiny. He maintains the resolve that he is prepared to endure whatever destiny brings, acknowledging that divine will is greater than himself; to the best of his intellectual capacity, he will only act in harmony with that divine will. He will never live in denial of that destiny-ordained supreme guide. He will extend this respect to all individuals in this world. He will strive to uplift them so that they too may evolve and become like him. He does not dismiss their sense of justice; however, he does not attach himself to their sense of justice either. He will possess such a comprehensive sense of purpose.

[GV1:P1268] The reason for stating that he neither denies nor aligns with their sense of justice is because the justice of both these groups (the Sanyasi and ordinary individuals) operates on two entirely different planes. Regardless of the circumstances, he does not reject them. Whatever accusations they may levy against him, his only thought will be, "You speak according to your level of understanding; what else can I do?" Even if everyone collectively confronts him, he will not lose his self-restraint. By enduring and forgiving in every possible manner, he will remain profoundly focused on guiding them towards the righteous path. He will not exhibit any trace of selfishness. He will not hesitate to strike and inflict pain if it serves to reform them. It is akin to the previously mentioned story of the wasp. The pain is inflicted purely out of an unyielding desire to transform the other into someone like himself. This does not imply that he will inflict severe physical agony. Rather, he will repeatedly agitate their mind until they undergo a complete transformation.

[GV1:P1269] However, a disciple who lacks the capacity to endure and comprehend this will be unable to accept it. He will simply ask to be told what to do. But if told, will he listen? He will not obey even a single word.

[GV1:P1270] When joining an institution, one must reflect upon the following: What are its aims and objectives? How far will its ultimate vision reach? What is the path I must embrace to align with it? Am I suitable to be a part of it? If I am suitable, what actions must I undertake for it? Must I perform those actions until the very end, or is it sufficient to do them partially? Or, having done them partially, am I engaging in any action that undermines it? If I do fall into such a situation, how should I rectify it? What must I do to prevent such flaws from affecting my actions? One must contemplate all these aspects. This principle applies universally, not just to an institution or a home. One must exercise such vigilance even in the most trivial matters.

[GV1:P1271] This can only be understood through practice and experience. Merely claiming, "I have visited the ashram; I have seen that house; I know that person is so-and-so's son; I know he only possesses this much wealth; so-and-so's wealth was acquired during such-and-such period," does not imply that his life is a complete success. Just because one has measured and calculated all these things does not mean it represents an exemplary life for him or the world. Failing to perceive any of this true essence, one might attempt to provide guidance based merely on the measuring yardstick of their limited knowledge. That is where you make a mistake. It stems from your lack of experience.
